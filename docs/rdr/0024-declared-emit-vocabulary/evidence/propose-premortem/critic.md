## 1. Prospective hindsight — the failure as accomplished fact

The feature shipped in week 0. By week 2, the first adopting team hit the whole-model cliff: declaring the one enum they cared about forced declarations for 37 other keys, so they generated `kind = "scalar"` stanzas for all of them (P-2). The lint output now said every emit key was declared, and the consumer team — reading "declared" as "validated" — deleted their golden table (P-11). The misspelled command value the RDR opened with was reintroduced in week 4 on a scalar-declared key and shipped with exit 0.

In week 5, a second model appended `dispositions` handling. Their stop rows were defaulted: the stop value reached the payload through a fallback row, not the selected row's authored cell, and the implementation reported `{}` for that key (P-7). The consumer skill's logic was `if dispositions[k] == "stop"`; `{}` made that false, the fallback prefix check had been deleted, and a compliance stop was printed and executed as a command. The skipped check read as a passed one — the precise sentence from the problem statement, now on the far side of the mitigation.

In week 6, the platform upgrade landed for non-adopters. Every resolve payload grew `"dispositions": {}` (P-5). Three consumer golden tables, byte-comparing payloads, went red across models that had never authored a declaration; the on-call classified it as a resolver regression and rolled the CLI back, un-shipping the guard for the adopters too.

The terminal incident came at quarter close. The consumer added a new close-out command; the model's enum lagged one deploy behind. At load, `emit_value_out_of_domain` refused the whole table — load refusals block both `lint` and `flow resolve` — and every route in that model, including forty untouched rows, was offline during the close-out window (P-9). The post-mortem's first line: "a validation feature designed to protect close-outs was the mechanism that blocked one." Its second line: the refusal that finally fired first was `unknown_emit_key` on an unrelated file, masking the real finding for ninety minutes (P-17).

## 2. Obstacle negation, claim by claim

**Claim 1 — whole-model strictness catches a typo'd key.** Negated two ways. (a) Mirror-typo: strictness checks rule keys against declared keys; it cannot know `nxet` is wrong if `[emit.nxet]` exists, and the most common authoring motion — copy the key from the rule into the declaration block — produces exactly that. Strictness proves declaration/usage *consistency*, which is weaker than the claimed *correctness*. (b) The trigger condition ("any declaration present") means a model with zero declarations gets zero key checking forever; the typo'd-key defect from the problem statement remains fully reachable for every non-adopter, so the claim holds only inside an opt-in island. The approach must answer: what makes the declaration block's spelling more trustworthy than the rule's, and what drives adoption past the cliff without the scalar dodge (P-1, P-2)?

**Claim 2 — both checks are single-rule; load is the right home; no cross-row analysis.** Negated. The checks as specified are single-rule, but the *useful* invariants over a declared vocabulary are not: an enum member no rule emits (dead stop token — a close-out path that cannot fire), a disposition token used by declarations but unreachable in the graph, and coverage of the domain by the table are all whole-model, graph-side properties. Declaring the vocabulary and then not feeding it to the graph-lint proof leaves the new metadata half-used: the table's routing is proven, its vocabulary's *liveness* is not — a reconstruction, one layer up, of the guard/answer split the RDR set out to close (P-3). Separately, "at load" silently means "on the authored form"; the proof and the executor consume the normalized model. If load-time validation and evaluation-time byte comparison look at different artifacts, single-rule decidability is true and irrelevant (P-4, expanded under Seed 3).

**Claim 3 — zero-declaration models byte-for-byte unaffected.** Negated by the approach's own text: `dispositions` is appended to the resolve success payload "`{}` when none" — that is, unconditionally. A zero-declaration model's *load and lint* may be unaffected; its *payload* is not, and the payload is what the motivating consumer byte-executes and what golden tables byte-compare. If declarations are "carried losslessly onto the normalized model," the normalized-export schema question must be answered for the empty case too. The claim as written is false; the true claim ("load/lint semantics unchanged; payload schema changes for everyone") has a corpus-wide blast radius the proposal has not priced (P-5).

**Claim 4 — appending `dispositions` is additive and safe.** Negated on three fronts. (a) This ecosystem's stated virtue is strictness; a consumer that mirrors it with a closed payload schema refuses the new field. (b) `{}` is indistinguishable from "tool predates the feature," so no consumer can treat absence-of-stop-disposition as proof-of-route; every correct consumer keeps the prefix fallback, defeating claim 8. (c) "The selected row's authored value" is undefined when the value arrives via default/merge; the natural short-circuit yields `{}` for exactly the rows (defaults, escapes) that history says get missed (P-6, P-7).

**Claim 5 — emit answers enumerable at authoring time.** Negated by any parameterized value: `stopped:<code>` with an open code family, or a command carrying an argument, cannot be a finite member list. The documented fallback is `scalar` — which is claim 7's escape hatch — so the keys most subject to churn and interpolation, i.e. the likeliest typo sites, are the ones that end up unguarded. Enumerability also creates a two-repo coupling: the domain now lives in the model but is defined by the consumer's command surface, with no stated sync mechanism, and the failure mode of drift is whole-table refusal (P-8, P-9).

**Claim 6 — dispositions per member, not per key.** The premise (one key's values split route/stop) is sound; the mechanism is under-specified. A member appearing in two disposition sub-arrays is not named as `malformed_emit_declaration`; absent that refusal, the resolve-time token lookup over a Go map of disposition sub-tables is iteration-order-dependent, i.e. nondeterministic per process. A validation feature that answers `route` or `stop` for the same input on different runs is worse than the prefix convention it replaces (P-10). Per-member declaration also forces full command enumeration merely to tag dispositions, compounding claim 5's burden.

**Claim 7 — `scalar` is an acceptable escape hatch.** Negated by interaction with claim 1's whole-model rule: strictness *manufactures demand* for `scalar` (bulk-declare to satisfy `unknown_emit_key`), and nothing in lint output, findings, or the normalized model distinguishes a domain-checked key from a scalar-waved one. The guarantee is not merely weakened at scalar keys; it is *misrepresented*, because "declarations present, lint green" now reads as vocabulary safety model-wide. A scalar key also has no disposition partition, so its stop values fall back to the prefix discipline inside a system that claims to have retired it (P-2, P-11).

**Claim 8 — verbatim model-authored tokens suffice.** Negated: the tool "never interprets" the token, so *someone* must, and that someone is each consumer, per model, by string comparison against a vocabulary the model author chose and may rename. The prefix discipline (`stopped:` on the value) has been replaced by a token discipline (`"stop"` in the payload) with strictly worse coordination properties: at least the prefix lived in the same string the executor already handled, whereas the token is a second channel that can drift independently. A model-side rename from `stop` to `halt` lints clean, flows through resolve verbatim, and turns every consumer's stop check into a silent route — the exact catastrophe class (stop executed as command) the RDR exists to prevent, now wearing the new feature as camouflage (P-12). The rejected reserved-prefix alternative was rejected for "standardizing the convention instead of replacing it" — but standardization across models is precisely the property claim 8 discards.

**Rejected alternatives, inverted.** (a) *Resolve-time validation* was rejected for proving only exercised rows; the symmetric cost of the chosen load-time whole-model refusal — one bad cell anywhere denies every route in the table, in production, with `flow resolve` itself blocked — is never priced, and it is the documented Seed-1 shipped defect (P-15, P-9). (b) *Tag-grammar reuse* was rejected to avoid amending a Final contract; the chosen approach amends the resolve envelope and interprets emit values via typed kinds, both plausibly governed by adjacent locked clauses that the proposal never cites (P-13). (c) *Reserved stop-prefix* was rejected as marking stops only; claim 8's failure shows the stop-marking half was the load-bearing half for the executor-safety property, and the chosen design provides it in a weaker (per-model, renamable) form (P-12).

## 3. Consumer artifacts that would have caught each failure

- **P-1 — `TestLoad_MirrorTypoDeclaration`:** model declares `[emit.nxet]` and a rule emits under `nxet`; assert the review question "what refuses this?" has an answer. (Expected outcome today: nothing refuses it; the test exists to force the design to say so out loud.)
- **P-2 / P-11 — journey "First declaration on a 40-key model":** an author adds one enum declaration to a large existing model and must reach green lint. Record every stanza they write. Acceptance criterion: the path of least resistance is not `kind = "scalar"` x39; or, if it is, a named finding (advisory tier) counts scalar-declared keys so the hollowing is visible.
- **P-3 — `TestLint_DeadEnumMember`:** declare `stop = ["stopped:aged-out"]`, author no rule that ever emits it, run the graph-lint gate; assert a finding (at minimum advisory) that the declared close-out token is unreachable.
- **P-4 — `TestNormalize_EmitValueProvenance`:** for every emit value present on the normalized model, assert it was validated at load (byte-identical to an authored, checked value). Run against a model exercising defaults, merges, and both-operator rows.
- **P-5 — `TestResolve_ZeroDeclPayloadGolden`:** byte-golden of the resolve success payload for a zero-declaration model, captured pre-feature, replayed post-feature. This single test falsifies claim 3 as written and forces the versioning decision at review time. Companion: byte-golden of the normalized-graph export for the same model.
- **P-6 — `TestConsumer_StrictPayloadDecode`:** decode the new payload with the motivating consumer's strictest decoder configuration; assert acceptance. And `TestConsumer_OldToolAmbiguity`: feed a pre-feature payload (no `dispositions`) to the new skill; assert its stop handling does not degrade to treating everything as a route.
- **P-7 — `TestResolve_DispositionViaDefaultRow`:** a stop value delivered by a default/fallback/merged row, not the selected row's own cell; assert `dispositions` carries the stop token. This is the single most important test in the set; it targets the Seed-2 class directly.
- **P-8 — journey "Declare the stopped-code family":** an author tries to declare a key whose stop values are `stopped:<code>` with codes minted per incident. Record whether the grammar can express it or the author lands on `scalar`. If `scalar`, the RDR must state that the motivating keys are outside the guarantee.
- **P-9 — journey "Consumer ships a new command first":** consumer adds command, model enum lags one deploy; measure blast radius. Acceptance criterion: a stated degraded mode, staged strictness, or an explicit RDR acceptance of whole-table refusal with an ops runbook.
- **P-10 — `TestLoad_DuplicateDispositionMembership`:** one member under both `route` and `stop`; assert `malformed_emit_declaration`. Plus a determinism harness: 100 resolves of the same input asserting a byte-identical `dispositions` payload.
- **P-12 — `TestConsumer_TokenRename`:** rename the model's `stop` token to `halt`; run the consumer's stop-detection; assert the rename is *caught somewhere* (consumer contract test, lint vocabulary pin, or declared-token registry). If nothing catches it, claim 8 is refuted by construction.
- **P-13 — artifact "Adjacent-clause audit":** a review checklist row quoting, verbatim, the locked clauses adjacent to "emit keys are NOT tag keys" — the uninterpreted-byte-equality clause and the resolve-envelope field-set clause — with a written disposition for each ("dispositions field is/is not an amendment; typed kinds do/do not interpret"). Named, quoted, checked off.
- **P-14 — `TestLoad_IntKindByteMismatch`:** declare `kind = "int"`, author `03`; assert the design's chosen behavior (refuse non-canonical form, or document that declared-valid ≠ matchable) with a test either way.
- **P-16 — `TestFindings_EmitRefusalsAreBlocking`:** assert all three new refusal codes surface in the blocking tier under both `lint` and `flow resolve`, never CLOSED/advisory, with exit codes checked.
- **P-17 — `TestLoad_RefusalOrdering`:** a model with both a pre-existing structural refusal and a new emit refusal; assert both findings surface in one pass (no masking short-circuit).

## 4. Refutation targets — the three seed classes applied here

**Seed 1 — the un-cited neighboring clause of a heavily-cited contract.** The proposal cites the locked clause "emit keys are NOT tag keys" to reject grammar reuse — the heavily-cited clause. Its neighbors go un-cited: (a) emit values are "fixed as uninterpreted, compared by byte equality" — yet `kind = int/bool` *interprets* values at load, creating the P-14 divergence where declared-validity and byte-matchability disagree; (b) the resolve success payload's field set — if the envelope is under the same locked contract (and the rejection of tag-reuse shows the team treats that contract as amendment-proof), then appending `dispositions` is itself the amendment the proposal elsewhere refuses to make. The analogous shipped defect: implement-time contract audit flags the envelope change, and the "additive field" is either walled off behind a flag (consumers never get it) or shipped as an unacknowledged amendment. Second instance of the class: the rejection of resolve-time validation never cites the neighboring operational clause of its own argument — that load refusals block *both* lint and resolve — which converts the chosen alternative into whole-table denial of service (P-9, P-15), the very control-flow the Seed-1 post-mortem describes ("refused whole tables on one unbound key").

**Seed 2 — an "additive only" claim defeated by evaluation order.** Two candidates. (a) `dispositions` is computed from "the selected row's authored value." Rows whose payload values arrive by default, merge, or fallback have no authored cell on the selected row; the natural implementation's early path (select row → read authored cells → map to tokens → return) never consults the default-injection step, so defaulted stops surface as `{}` and the executor routes them (P-7). This is the seed verbatim: the default/escape row is the one the ordering silently drops. (b) Load-check ordering: "with zero declarations, loads exactly as today" is an additive claim about the *acceptance set*, but inserting the emit checks into the load pipeline changes which refusal fires first for models that opt in, and a whole-file `unknown_emit_key` can mask the structural finding behind it (P-17). And per P-5, the additive claim is already false at the payload layer: appending a field to a byte-compared envelope turns passing consumer goldens into failures — an acceptance turned into a refusal by an "additive" change, one system boundary out.

**Seed 3 — a load-bearing premise about the grammar, never checked against the normalizer.** The load-bearing premise here is: *every emit value the executor can receive is an emit value that load-time validation saw.* That is a claim about the normalizer, and the brief never checks it. Validation is specified at LOAD over authored `[rule.emit]` cells; the proof runs on the normalized model, declarations are "carried losslessly" onto it, and resolve serves from it. If the normalizer synthesizes emit entries (envelope defaults merged into rows), canonicalizes values (case, whitespace, quoting), or — per the seed's own precedent — emits *two* atoms where the author wrote one compound form, then post-normalization emit values exist that were never domain-checked, and "carried losslessly" is doing unexamined work in the same sentence. The precise analogue of the seed's two-atoms defect: an authored emit cell the grammar treats as one value that the normalizer splits or rewrites into a form outside the declared member list — lints clean (declarations checked against authored form), resolves to an out-of-domain byte string, executes verbatim. The artifact that kills this class is P-4 (`TestNormalize_EmitValueProvenance`), and it must run over the normalizer's full emit-affecting behavior inventory, which the RDR must first *write down* — the inventory's absence is the finding.

## Disposition

The core mechanism — per-key declarations, load-time domain refusal, disposition surfacing — survives: every finding above is answerable by specification tightening, tier pinning, ordering tests, and honest re-scoping of claims 3/7/8, without switching approaches. But the proposal as written makes at least four statements that are false or undefined (claim 3's byte-for-byte assertion; claim 4's authored-value definition under defaults; duplicate disposition membership; validation artifact vs. execution artifact), and its safety story for stops still terminates in an unchecked string comparison, now over tokens instead of prefixes. PASS is conditional on folding the mitigations enumerated in the return packet; P-7 and P-4 are the two that re-create the original catastrophe class if skipped.
